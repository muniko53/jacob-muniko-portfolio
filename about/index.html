About

A working method, not a personal brand.

I am Jacob Muniko, a technology builder in Karatina, Kenya. I work across web applications, local AI tools, Windows software, and automation. Most of what I know came from building something, watching it fail, and fixing the part that was actually wrong.

Background

Where the work started

I am in my final year of a Bachelor of Education (Arts) at Karatina University, in the School of Education and Social Sciences, Department of Curriculum Technology and Management. My teaching subjects are Business Studies and Geography, and I expect to graduate around November 2027. The programme taught me to look at the people, the incentives, and the constraint before the technology. The projects taught me that the technology still has to work.

Linklet started as a campus marketplace: students needed a safer, more local way to buy and sell than scattered WhatsApp groups and noticeboards. Building it took me through authentication, real-time messaging, admin tools, database migrations, payments integration, and deployment. It is the project that made the rest of the work possible.

From there I moved into automation and AI: scheduled reports, provider fallbacks, approval gates, and local-first tools. FilePilot came from a practical annoyance, a full C: drive and no clear answer about what was using it. MICA came from a different question: what would a useful desktop assistant look like if it had to ask before it changed anything?

How I build

The method

Start with the constraint. A problem statement that names the user, the failure, and the environment is worth more than a feature list. If I cannot explain what breaks without the software, I am not ready to build it.

Build the smallest complete thing. Not a demo, not a mock-up: the smallest version that a real person can use end to end. Then test the failure path before adding another feature.

Make the safe path the default. This matters most in FilePilot and MICA. FilePilot plans before it deletes and revalidates every item. MICA asks before a consequential action and fails closed when it cannot verify what it is acting on.

Write down what is uncertain. A known limitation is part of the product. Hiding it only moves the surprise to the user.

AI and the work

How the tools fit

I use AI coding tools in my workflow, including OpenCode, Claude, and Antigravity. They are useful for scaffolding, refactors, and the parts of a codebase that are tedious to hold in your head. They are not the author of the work.

I define the architecture, review the changes, run the tests, and stay accountable for what ships. When a tool produces something I cannot explain, I do not keep it. That rule is the difference between a faster builder and a less careful one.

That is also why the case files separate implemented features from planned ones. A tool that can generate a convincing page can also generate a convincing claim. The verification ledger in this repository exists to stop that.

Principles

Four rules I keep

  1. The demo is not the product. A system has to survive real data, a slow connection, and a user who does the unexpected thing.
  2. Make the safe path the default. Destructive actions need a plan, a confirmation, and a way back.
  3. Show the evidence. Screenshots, code, test results, and limitations. Not adjectives.
  4. Finish the handover. Source, setup notes, and a walkthrough. A project is not done when it runs on my machine.
